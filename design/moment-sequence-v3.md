# What Happens to a Moment — playable sequence draft

This draft follows the latest branch architecture and reporter-on-the-ground voice. It is a writing and dependency specification, not yet a replacement for the playable HTML. The tables are author-facing. Players see the dispatch, question and action with a short description; effects and requirements become visible through consequences, not scoring hints.

## The political year

Turn 1 → leave (2A–4A) or continue (2B–4B) → schools (5) → hostile label (6) → wider issues (7) → internal authority (8) → institutional access (9) → competition (10) → protection (11) → elections (12).

No branch is intrinsically successful. Leaving exchanges physical leverage for room to build. Staying exchanges time and public goodwill for sustained mobilisation and earlier institution-building. Turn 1D keeps the camp for the alliance meeting; it does not silently assume agreement to indefinite occupation.

## Turn 1 — The breakthrough

**LIVE · FROM THE PROTEST SITE**

“The resignation has brought more students onto the streets. Groups are still arriving, and new demands are being raised. But police are turning back food deliveries, restricting nearby transport and stopping new arrivals after dusk. Officers have been filming students and recording names and colleges. A senior official has offered talks—if the tents come down first. The crowd is waiting for the organisers’ response.”

**What do you tell them?**

| Action | Immediate benefit | Cost or negative externality | Requirement created → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. “We won. Bring the tents down.” End the occupation and pursue the remaining commitments. | Ends exposure at the camp; gives participants a clear victory. | The crowd disperses before implementation; some students call it premature. | Establish what was promised and who follows it → 3A. Opens demobilisation. |
| B. “We leave when the settlement is complete.” Publish the outstanding commitments and an exit condition. | Keeps pressure tied to a defined objective. | Police restrictions continue; allies must sustain an occupation they may not have planned. | Logistics, consent and a credible settlement timetable → 2B, 4B. |
| C. “This is bigger than a resignation.” Ask the crowd to stay for a second charter. | Gives the growing crowd a continuing purpose. | Government can allege moving goalposts; new claims may outrun expertise and consent. | Choose the charter now and secure a mandate → 2B, 3B, 7. |
| D. “The groups who built this decide together.” Call the alliance into an overnight meeting. | Gives partners a real say before committing them. | A divided meeting can delay your answer while supplies run down. | Record how partners decide and who can negotiate → 2B, 3B, 4B. |

### After 1A — What assurance can your team accept?

“Before the last tents come down, your negotiators call. The Government says the cases will be withdrawn. They need to know what they can accept on your behalf.”

| Action | Benefit | Cost | Requirement → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| Written commitment from the responsible authority | Creates a named documentary reference. | Negotiations take longer; the document is not itself a withdrawal order. | Check dates, jurisdiction and implementation → 3A. |
| Senior minister’s public assurance | Makes the promise visible. | Political speech may not become an administrative instruction. | Preserve the recording and obtain an operative order → 3A. |
| A trusted intermediary’s assurance | Can resolve an impasse quickly. | The relationship carries the promise; the record may be disputed. | Maintain the channel and document follow-up → 3A. |
| The resignation is enough | Ends the occupation immediately. | No further case-withdrawal agreement has been secured. | Begin a fresh claim rather than alleging a broken agreement → 3A. |

### After 1C — Widening is a decision chain

Keep these as short consecutive scenes inside Turn 1, not additional numbered turns. Each answer changes the next scene. The player has announced an intention to widen; the movement has not yet consented to a programme or acquired the capacity to deliver it.

**Flow:** Bigger than what? → Who builds it? → How does it work? → 11:40 PM: first disagreement → Turn 2B.

#### 1C.1 — Bigger than what?

**LIVE · BESIDE THE STAGE**

“The call to continue has been cheered. But representatives are now asking what tomorrow’s demands will be. Some came for education reform. Others say the police response has changed what this protest is about.”

**What are you asking people to stay for?**

| Action | Direct benefit | Cost / possible adverse reaction | Requirement → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Reform the education system. Extend the campaign to examinations, universities and schools. | Builds on a recognisable mandate. | Some participants seeking wider change may see the programme as too narrow. | Specify reforms and recruit education expertise → 5, 7, 10. |
| B. Defend student rights. Include policing, protest rights, campus democracy and treatment of students. | Connects the original demand to what participants have experienced. | Makes institutional authority and civil liberties part of the argument; hesitant supporters may withdraw. | Legal expertise, participant protection and agreed principles → 4B, 6, 11. |
| C. Organise around young people’s lives. Include employment, housing, safety and discrimination. | Opens a much larger potential constituency. | Claims multiply faster than competence; allies may disagree on their political causes. | Recruit issue specialists and decide priorities → 7, 8, 10. |
| D. Make democratic accountability the mandate. Support people unable to make institutions respond. | Offers a broad explanation of the breakthrough and a basis for solidarity. | Raises the political cost of participation and expectations far beyond education. | Define principles, limits and reciprocal relationships → 6, 7, 9. |

Education-system work does not automatically confer an ideological position. Rights and accountability routes create explicit commitments; they do not automatically reduce moral worth or establish hostility to the state.

#### 1C.2 — Who are you asking to build it?

**REPORT · THE COORDINATION TENT**

“The proposed programme is circulating. Groups that brought lawyers, transport and students to the original protest want to know where they fit. New volunteers are asking where to sign up.”

**Who will build this next phase?**

| Action | Direct benefit | Cost / possible adverse reaction | Requirement → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Expand CJP itself. Invite people and organisations into a CJP-led movement. | Retains a recognisable identity and point of coordination. | Existing allies may see a shared victory becoming someone else’s recruitment drive. | Agree affiliation terms and authority over the shared name → midnight test, 4B, 8. |
| B. Keep the existing alliance together. Make the original partners the core of the next phase. | Retains working relationships and available infrastructure. | Partners bring their own affiliations, histories and priorities. | Secure renewed consent; define participation and conflicts of interest → 4B, 6, 9. |
| C. Open a new coalition. Invite student groups, creators, independent organisers and local movements. | Opens access to new constituencies and skills. | Invitations do not establish trust; verification and coordination become harder. | Establish entry rules and negotiate obligations → 3B, 8, 11. |
| D. Let the camp constitute the movement. Invite people and groups on the ground to form an assembly. | Gives participants a direct route to representation. | The camp does not represent every supporter; organising a mandate takes time under pressure. | Register participants and agree representation before claiming assembly authority → midnight test, 3B, 4B. |

This choice defines who is invited, not who controls decisions. It does not silently select the next governance model. Existing allies have initial relationships; a newly invited coalition does not instantly inherit those relationships.

#### 1C.3 — How does this thing actually work?

**FROM THE MEETING ROOM**

“Volunteers need a contact for tomorrow’s transport. Campus groups want to issue statements. Journalists have asked for a spokesperson. The new programme still has no agreed way to make a decision.”

**How will the movement act?**

| Action | Direct benefit | Cost / possible adverse reaction | Requirement → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Establish a national executive. Appoint state and campus coordinators. | Creates a fast, identifiable chain of command. | Authority concentrates around identifiable leaders; their mistakes become the movement’s responsibility. | Define appointments, limits and reporting → midnight test, 3B, 8. |
| B. Build a federation. Let units organise within a common charter. | Accommodates local initiative and difference. | A local intervention can carry national consequences without national approval. | Define which decisions belong to units and which require agreement → midnight test, 7, 8. |
| C. Build a membership organisation. Register members and elect representatives. | Creates a route to durable, accountable representation. | Enrolment and elections cost time and money; founders must eventually accept their outcomes. | Complete a roll, election and interim mandate → 3B, funding, 8, 12. |
| D. Form an alliance council. Participating organisations nominate delegates. | Connects partner infrastructure to shared decisions. | CJP shares ownership; party-linked delegates can expose it to proxy allegations. | Ratify council membership, voting and reserved decisions → midnight test, 4B, 9. |
| E. Keep it open for now. Let groups act while the movement finds its shape. | Preserves initiative without waiting for a constitution. | Nobody can reliably bind the whole movement; conflicting statements accumulate. | Carry unresolved authority into every joint decision → 3B, 4B, 8. |

Five choices are justified here: “no settled structure” must be a real route, not an accidental penalty imposed on another model. It grants neither a national mandate nor a functioning alliance council.

**Combination checks:** an invited camp assembly followed by a national executive must visibly disappoint the expectation of participant control. A CJP expansion followed by an alliance council requires sharing the authority first claimed for CJP. Open-coalition membership requires registration and verification. These combinations remain possible, but their terms must be acknowledged in the next scene.

#### 1C.4 — 11:40 PM · First disagreement

**LIVE · OUTSIDE THE COORDINATION TENT**

“Representatives have been meeting for nearly two hours. One group wants withdrawal of all student cases added to tomorrow’s demands. Another wants employment included. A third says it will walk out if party representatives are allowed into the coordinating body.

“The press conference is scheduled for midnight. Journalists are already outside asking who is now leading the movement.”

Adapt the dispute to the selected mandate: if employment is already included, the disagreement is whether it becomes tomorrow’s priority. If case withdrawal is already a demand, the disagreement is whether it must be settled before any other negotiation. Do not ask the player to add something already agreed.

**What can you authorise before midnight?**

This is a test of the chosen structure, not a fresh menu of constitutions. Display the relevant three actions below. Their outcomes establish whether the structure has authority, is still being built, or has already been overridden.

| Chosen structure | Plausible immediate actions | Benefit, cost and dependency |
|---|---|---|
| National executive | **Decide within the executive’s stated remit.** / **Postpone the disputed points and consult partners.** / **Override the remit and announce a wider position.** | Decision supplies speed but makes leadership own the result. Consultation preserves consent at the cost of the deadline. Override creates an explicit mandate breach and partner grievance → 4B, 8. |
| Federation | **Announce only the common charter.** / **Delay while units approve the disputed additions.** / **Let units state their own positions and identify them as local.** | Common ground limits tonight’s claim. Ratification costs time. Local statements preserve autonomy but create a fragmented public position → 7, 8. No invented national veto. |
| Membership organisation, not yet elected | **Give a temporary committee a narrow, recorded remit.** / **Delay the disputed decision until registration and representation are agreed.** / **Let founders decide provisionally, with a published review date.** | Interim action can meet a deadline but is not a member vote. Delay protects the promised process while the camp waits. Founder discretion creates an obligation to submit the decision for review → 3B, 8. |
| Alliance council | **Ask confirmed delegates to agree a joint statement.** / **Publish only agreed points and record dissent.** / **Issue a CJP-only statement without claiming alliance consent.** | Joint agreement lends authority but partners can withhold assent. Recording dissent limits the claim. A separate statement preserves CJP’s voice while visibly surrendering collective ownership → 4B, 9. If delegates lack mandates, first action becomes a proposal awaiting their organisations’ approval. |
| Open / no settled structure | **Announce only what the original mobilisation agreed.** / **Let groups address the press separately.** / **Claim a common position without a mandate.** | A narrow statement buys time. Separate statements preserve energy but expose disagreement. A claim without consent is fast and politically consequential: other groups may reject it publicly → 3B, 4B, 8. |

After the choice, show a short dispatch naming who agreed, who reserved their position, and whether the press conference went ahead or was delayed. Never convert “asked for agreement” into automatic consent.

### Consequences and state for the widening chain

Record four separate decisions: `charter`, `participant_base`, `structure_plan`, `first_dispute_response`. Also record `mandate_status`, `unresolved_disagreements`, `interim_authority`, and any deadline for ratification. Record completed governance separately from intended governance.

- A declared membership plan is not `MEMBERSHIP_GOVERNANCE` until registration and representation are completed.
- An alliance council is operational only when delegates are mandated and its rules accepted.
- A camp assembly needs a defined electorate and scope; it does not claim to represent absent supporters automatically.
- A federation needs a common charter and named local decision rights.
- No-structure play remains viable, with fast initiatives and unreliable collective commitments.

Keep direct gains modest and tied to actual commitments. Announcing a broad programme may attract interest; it does not grant organisers, legal capacity or an automatic rise in public legitimacy. Choosing successive follow-ups must not make widening a way to collect four turns of bonuses inside Turn 1.

Do not add a random event after each follow-up. Store these decisions as probability modifiers for the scheduled Turn 2B reaction. A comprehensible charter, fulfilled promises about representation and an authorised statement favour a credible public explanation. Unmandated expansion and visibly excluded partners favour adverse or mixed reception. Neither reaction is certain.

**Later callbacks:**

- **2B:** asks whether the announced programme still justifies remaining; uses the actual charter and midnight disagreement.
- **3B:** tests implementation of the chosen structure rather than asking the player to choose it again. Membership players must complete registration and representation; councils must obtain mandates; federations must settle decision boundaries; executives must establish appointments and reporting. Open-structure players can stay open or begin building a structure, with time and consent costs.
- **4B:** a jointly mandated delegation requires an actual joint mandate. An executive can send its own delegates but cannot claim the alliance authorised them.
- **6:** party-participation rules and real affiliations affect hostile relabelling, separately from actual control.
- **7:** the original charter is a commitment, not a disposable label. Changing it invokes the decision procedure the player established.
- **8:** the author of the disputed statement and available response follow the chosen model. The midnight precedent returns if the leadership now wants a different rule.
- **9:** partners can condition or veto a council’s escalation according to its agreed rules; independent executive authority does not confer an alliance mandate.
- **11:** reciprocal relationships determine whether allies provide lawyers and protection. Their help is not permanently unlocked by inviting them once.
- **12:** member rules and shared control can retain the institution while individuals take political offers. A personal announcement cannot dispose of collective assets without consequence.

These follow-ups apply whenever the player first chooses to widen, including at 2B. If a charter or structure already exists, ask whether to keep it or propose a change through its established process. Never replay the chain as if the movement had no history.

## Turn 2A — The protest ends. The protests don’t.

**REPORT · CAMPUSES ACROSS THE COUNTRY**

“The tents are gone, but their example is travelling. Students protesting fees, examinations and recruitment are citing the resignation as proof that pressure works. Your inbox is filling with requests for lawyers, publicity and someone who knows how to organise. Your volunteers are back in class.”

**What do you build for the people now approaching you?**

| Action | Immediate benefit | Cost or negative externality | Requirement created → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Give other struggles your platform. | Makes overlooked cases visible quickly. | Requests compete for attention; your public association may exceed what you can verify. | Editorial checks and a way to hand cases onward → 4A, 6. |
| B. Set up a protest-support desk. | Coordinates the lawyers and organisers offering help. | A desk can receive more cases than it can serve. | Intake, consent and a staffed rota; choose an initial service → 3A, 11. |
| C. Build an education working group. | Concentrates volunteers around a recognisable mandate. | Wider youth campaigns find help elsewhere. | Recruit subject expertise and define membership → 5, 7. |
| D. Keep the original alliance working. | Retains existing networks and institutional contacts. | Partners expect influence; visible party ties may deter unaffiliated students. | Participation rules and reciprocal support → 4A, 8, 9. |

**Optional organising detail for B/C/D:** appoint coordinators now, elect a member committee after registration, or use a council of participating organisations. Appointment is fast but creates personal authority; elections require a real roll and time; a council supplies reach but gives partners shared control. This provides the demobilisation branch a route to governance before Turn 8. A player choosing amplification can begin the same process through parent committees at Turn 5.

**External event slot E1:** a new student confrontation receives national attention. The draw controls how your contribution is received—not whether an unrelated protest becomes violent because of your choice. Verified support can be welcomed; your association can attract disputed claims; established leaders can take the credit; nothing unexpected may happen.

## Turn 3A — The promise comes due

**FOLLOW-UP · SIX WEEKS AFTER THE PROTEST**

“Students say the original cases are still open. Some report online targeting and worry about the police records made at the site. Their messages are now reaching the organisers: who is following this up?”

Insert the actual assurance: signed commitment; minister’s recorded words; intermediary’s account; or no withdrawal agreement. Never call an absent promise a broken promise.

**How do you get the cases moving now that the crowd is gone?**

| Action | Immediate benefit | Cost or negative externality | Requirement created → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Announce a date to return to the streets. | Creates a deadline backed by remembered mobilisation. | A weak response exposes the threat; students face renewed visibility. | Confirm participation and organise protection → 6, 11. |
| B. Assign a legal team and build a case tracker. | Converts individual accounts into actionable files. | Verification is slow, and public disclosure can expose students. | Consent, confidential records, lawyers and staff; a support desk speeds this up → 5, 11. |
| C. Ask alliance partners to intervene. | Uses relationships with lawyers and political offices. | Credit and dependence shift towards those delivering relief. | Keep partners involved beyond this crisis → 6, 8, 9. |
| D. Send a small delegation to the responsible office. | Creates a direct route for follow-up. | A private channel is hard to explain if commitments remain undocumented. | Record decisions and establish reporting to students → 9, 11. |

**Aftermath:** the responsible office begins moving some cases; unresolved cases remain in the record. Timing and coverage depend on capacity. The game does not certify why the Government acted. Avoid a factual assertion that it wants to keep you alive. A reporter may quote competing interpretations, clearly as interpretations.

**Implementation rule:** without a live alliance, C becomes “ask for new introductions”, with delay and uncertain help. Building a tracker starts work; it does not instantly complete legal infrastructure.

## Turn 4A — Somebody else gets there first

**LIVE · OUTSIDE ANOTHER CAMPUS**

“Local organisers were here before your team. Lawyers have secured a hearing, and elected representatives are pressing for the detained students’ release. The crowd knows who turned up. Your supporters are asking whether you are coming.”

**What do you bring that is not already here?**

| Action | Immediate benefit | Cost or negative externality | Requirement created → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Call a national mobilisation behind the students. | Can widen pressure beyond the local dispute. | Competes with local strategy and consumes your organising capacity. | Obtain local consent and supply transport and legal support → 6, 11. |
| B. Join the existing coordination team. | Adds your reach to an operating response. | You share decisions and appear alongside politically affiliated actors. | Honour the joint plan and reciprocate → 8, 9. |
| C. Offer verified publicity and practical support. | Fills a specific gap without displacing local leaders. | The useful work may be credited to others. | Ask what is needed and deliver it → 5, 10. |
| D. Keep your team on its existing programme. | Protects commitments already made. | A previously claimed youth constituency may read absence as abandonment. | Explain the boundary; broad-charter choices make this harder → 7, 10. |

## Turn 2B — Day five: “You already won”

**LIVE · THE OCCUPATION**

“The crowd remains large. Ministers now say the organisers are refusing to accept victory. Television crews are asking commuters about disruption. Inside the barricades, volunteers are rationing supplies. Some allies say they agreed to a protest, not an indefinite occupation.”

**What now justifies asking people to stay?**

| Action | Immediate benefit | Cost or negative externality | Requirement created → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Publish the unfinished commitments and an exit timetable. | Makes continued pressure comprehensible. | A narrower settlement may disappoint people drawn by wider promises. | Maintain the camp and report progress against specific demands → 4B, 5. |
| B. Put a second charter to the camp. | Offers a continuing programme. | Cannot assume allies or newcomers endorse it. | If widening for the first time, enter the widening chain. Otherwise seek amendments through the existing procedure → 3B, 7. |
| C. Let participating organisations negotiate the demands. | Gives partners a stake in sustaining the camp. | Disagreement may slow the response and reduce CJP’s control. | Establish a binding joint process → 3B, 4B. |
| D. Extend the occupation into another public assembly. | Demonstrates a willingness to sustain pressure. | Increases logistical strain, disruption and exposure to police action. | Arrange safety, legal response and a clear message → 4B, 6. |

**E1, branch version:** restrictions provoke sympathy / disruption dominates coverage / core commitment rises while cautious participants withdraw / the story does not shift. A clear unfinished settlement, trusted allies and public credibility favour sympathetic coverage. Unexplained widening and fractured alliances favour adverse coverage. No draw makes repression deserved.

## Turn 3B — Who speaks for this crowd?

**Who gets that authority?**

| Action | Immediate benefit | Cost or negative externality | Requirement created → return |
|---|---|---|---|

| B. Create a council of participating organisations. | Joins infrastructure to shared authority. | Party-linked partners acquire real influence. | Voting rules, conflicts of interest and withdrawal procedures → 4B, 8, 9. |

## Turn 4B — Separate invitations

**DEVELOPING · TALKS OFFERED**

“The Government has invited selected representatives to talks. Some groups keeping the camp supplied have been left off the list. Officials are drawing a distinction between ‘students’ and ‘political elements’. Your partners want to know whether you will enter without them.”

**On whose mandate do you negotiate?**

| Action | Immediate benefit | Cost or negative externality | Requirement created → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Send the CJP leadership alone. | Gets a delegation into the room quickly. | Excluded partners may stop contributing. | Explain concessions and repair trust; alliance capacity can be lost → 8, 9, 11. |
| B. Insist on including every participating organisation. | Defends the coalition’s standing. | Talks may stall while camp costs continue. | Keep partners and supplies committed → 5, 9. |
| C. Send a jointly mandated delegation. | Allows a smaller team to negotiate for a wider body. | Delegates must return for approval of disputed terms. | Requires an operating council or completed assembly; otherwise unavailable → 8, 9. |
| D. Refuse the invitation and hold another assembly. | Retains the occupation as leverage. | Invites another confrontation without resolving the exit question. | Participation, protection and a final exit decision → 5, 6. |

**E2:** the excluded groups accept the mandate / withdraw support / remain but publicly dissent / talks proceed without a new rupture. Existing governance and reciprocity improve the odds; previous unilateral decisions worsen them.

**Reconvergence must be shown, not assumed:** conclude with a concrete end to the occupation: agreed phased withdrawal, a published settlement with monitoring, or a visibly exhausted closure without agreement. The player sees which commitments remain unresolved. At most one brief exit confirmation is needed; no invisible camp disappearance between Turns 4 and 5.

## Turn 5 — Build something between the protests

**REPORT · SCHOOL THEEK KARO**

“School photographs are arriving from across the country: damaged rooms, missing facilities, unanswered complaints. Parents who never joined the protest are sending material. Your team has opened another folder. It is already filling faster than anyone can read it.”

For the demobilised branch: this rebuilds participation after dispersal. For the occupation branch: this tests whether the movement can work beyond confrontation. Carry unfinished cases and exhausted volunteers into the scene.

**What happens after somebody sends a report?**

| Action | Immediate benefit | Cost or negative externality | Requirement created → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Publish a weekly collection of verified stories. | Makes failures visible and invites wider participation. | Attention can outrun follow-up; families may expect remedies you cannot deliver. | Consent, verification and clear promises → 6, 10. |
| B. Build a district evidence register. | Makes reports usable in institutional arguments. | Records become stale without staff and repeated checking. | Fund research, corrections and secure case handling → 9, 10, 11. |
| C. Help parents form local committees. | Creates a local presence and continuing participation. | Every committee needs training and a decision process. | Define membership, responsibilities and escalation → 8, 10. |
| D. Follow selected cases through to repair. | Demonstrates concrete service and institutional follow-through. | Serves fewer cases and depends on offices that control implementation. | Caseworkers, documented commitments and honest reporting → 9, 11. |

Building these services does not set an ideological identity. Explaining failures through a wider political argument is a separate choice.

### After Turn 5 — The accountant calls

Use the existing ₹4.7 lakh / seven-week runway scene. Crowdfunding creates dependence on audience response; dues create obligations to members; grants restrict eligible work; a donor concentrates financial dependence; volunteer-only work narrows deliverable promises; party support couples useful capacity to political expectations. Money received is not money available for every purpose. Return to these obligations in Turns 8–12.

## Turn 6 — They give you a new name

**REPORT · THE NAME IS TRAVELLING**

“A hostile label for the movement is spreading through television clips and messaging groups. What began as an irreverent student identity is being recast as an accusation of anti-state politics. Your most committed supporters want to reclaim it. Parents are calling local organisers to ask what their children have joined.”

**What do you say—and what do you ask people to do?**

| Action | Immediate benefit | Cost or negative externality | Requirement created → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Reclaim the label at a public meeting. | Gives committed supporters a shared response. | Amplifies the association some students fear. | Protect participants and explain who remains welcome → 7, 11. |
| B. Issue a factual rebuttal and disclose your affiliations. | Offers evidence of your actual decisions and relationships. | Can prolong the controversy; a disclosure may reveal uncomfortable ties. | Maintain accurate public rules, not a one-off denial → 8, 9. |
| C. Keep the next campaign focused on school outcomes. | Gives hesitant supporters concrete work to join. | Does not answer every accusation; targeted students may feel unsupported. | Deliver results while maintaining participant protection → 10, 11. |
| D. State an explicit democratic-rights position. | Gives the movement a clearer political argument. | A higher political commitment may narrow participation. | Agree principles, educate organisers and accept difficult issues → 7, 8. |

**E3:** the accusation fails to travel / becomes the dominant frame / strengthens the core while reducing breadth / makes little difference. School delivery, transparent party rules and perceived independence modify reception. Sustained confrontation supplies material for a hostile frame but never proves its truth. Track actual independence separately from the public interpretation.

## Turn 8 — Whose movement?

**INTERNAL · A STATEMENT IN YOUR NAME**

“An intervention on a divisive youth issue has gone out under the movement’s name. Some organisers want it withdrawn. Others say withdrawing it would betray the people affected. The press is asking which statement represents the movement.”

The author must follow the actual structure: appointed spokesperson, autonomous group, elected representative, or alliance partner. Do not invent an elected convenor for a movement without membership.

**Who has the authority to settle this?**

| Action | Immediate benefit | Cost or negative externality | Requirement created → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Have the founders issue an interim position. | Produces an immediate answer. | Can override structures or alienate volunteers. | State the limits and submit to later review → 9, 12. |
| B. Use the elected body’s agreed procedure. | Gives the dispute an accountable route. | Hearings take time and may rule against founders. | Requires a completed membership/election process; unavailable otherwise → 12. |
| C. Refer it to the functioning alliance council. | Keeps partners inside a shared decision. | Affiliated organisations acquire influence over the answer. | Requires a live council and sufficient trust; unavailable after collapse → 9, 12. |
| D. Open a time-limited public consultation. | Lets participants speak where no mechanism exists. | Followers are not a verified electorate; organised blocs can dominate. | Say whether advice is binding and who decides afterward → 12. |

No instant elections. Where structures were never built, the interim response may start a later process, but cannot pretend that process already has authority.

## Turn 9 — Come inside

**DEVELOPING · AN OFFICIAL INVITATION**

“The ministry wants to hear the movement’s proposals. It is willing to recognise you as an independent stakeholder. Several allies are not invited. Officials want a photograph after the meeting; your members want to know what you will be authorised to agree.”

**What are your terms for entering?**

| Action | Immediate benefit | Cost or negative externality | Requirement created → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Decline and publish your demands outside. | Retains distance from the ministry’s process. | Other groups may shape decisions without you. | Build another route to implementation → 10, 11. |
| B. Attend through a private delegation. | Enables detailed, potentially candid negotiation. | Members cannot readily verify the exchange; either side can exploit the photograph. | Report back and track commitments → 10, 11. |
| C. Publish your mandate and an attributed meeting record. | Makes claims and follow-up inspectable. | Officials may dispute the account and restrict future access. | Verify attribution, handle corrections and fund follow-up → 10, 11. |
| D. Attend under an agreed alliance mandate. | Brings a broader constituency behind the delegation. | You cannot concede on your own; excluded partners may reject the terms. | Requires an active mandate; negotiate one first if absent → 11, 12. |

**E4:** recognition expands credibility / the photograph becomes a co-option story / credibility rises with one audience and falls with another / routine meeting. Transparent rules, reported funding, sustained independence and previous follow-through shift the odds. Separately, publication may create an operational access dispute. Neither reaction is guaranteed.

## Turn 10 — Everyone has learnt

**REPORT · THE COMPETITION FOR YOUTH**

“Party organisers now arrive with lawyers and elected representatives. Some of your demands appear in their programmes; others have become Government announcements. Students who once messaged you are taking their cases elsewhere. Your volunteers ask what the next year’s work is for.”

**What should people still come to you for?**

| Action | Immediate benefit | Cost or negative externality | Requirement created → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. The ability to bring people together in public. | Concentrates effort on collective pressure. | An unconvincing turnout exposes the gap between audience and mobilisation. | Maintain local organisers, consent and protection → 11, 12. |
| B. Evidence and a public charter that tests every party. | Gives adoption a continuing standard. | Weights and rankings are contested; partners may dispute the results. | Requires existing evidence for a credible launch; otherwise publish a limited draft → 12. |
| C. A local network that follows problems through. | Makes the organisation useful between crises. | Staff and funds are tied to slow, uneven work. | Requires active committees or caseworkers; start smaller if absent → 11, 12. |
| D. An independent route into political representation. | Gives supporters a possible electoral direction. | Creates candidate, money and policy obligations before any seat is won. | Consult members and build a viable organisation → 12. |

This choice allocates effort; it does not grant the claimed capability. Outcomes explicitly test the infrastructure already built.

## Turn 11 — Your people need you

**URGENT · AFTER THE RALLY**

“Members have been attacked. Their families are at the police station, where an FIR has not yet been registered. They need protection now. Your local organiser has sent one message: who can you get here?”

**What can you put into motion today?**

| Action | Immediate benefit | Cost or negative externality | Requirement created → return |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Activate your legal response. | Puts documents and representation behind the complaint. | Legal work takes staff and cannot guarantee immediate protection. | Requires operational legal capacity, not just a spreadsheet → 12. |
| B. Activate the alliance’s collective response. | Combines contacts and practical help. | Partners share the credit and may set conditions. | Requires live relationships and reciprocal trust → 12. |
| C. Escalate through an institutional contact. | Can reach an official with authority quickly. | Access may fail when the request is politically difficult. | Requires a functioning channel, not a historical meeting → 12. |
| D. Mobilise publicly outside the police station. | Creates visible urgency. | Exposes participants and tests whether anyone will come. | Requires reachable participants, coordination and safety arrangements → 12. |

**Fallback if all four are unavailable:** “Stay with the families and seek emergency outside help.” Always playable. Calls go out to new lawyers and organisations; delay and dependence are explicit. No route strands the player on four locked options.

**E5:** response is timely / institutions delay or resist / partial relief arrives but protection remains incomplete / no new complication. Capabilities change speed and coverage. Randomness must not turn a nonexistent legal team into an effective one or erase documented infrastructure. Avoid treating lawful protection as a guaranteed reward for correct play.

## Turn 12 — Elections organise the space

**REPORT · BEFORE THE ELECTION**

“Parties are approaching your organisers. Some want endorsements, others offer individual roles. Your demands are circulating in campaign speeches. Supporters are asking whether the movement will stand, bargain or stay outside. Several leaders want an answer before they respond to offers.”

**What will you commit the movement to?**

| Action | Immediate benefit | Cost or negative externality | Requirement created → ending |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Field independent candidates in selected seats. | Tests a direct route to representation. | Diverts people and money; governing disagreements become electoral choices. | Requires organisation, participation, agenda, usable funding and actual independence. |
| B. Seek public commitments from every political formation. | Turns demands into an explicit bargaining agenda. | Agreements may be symbolic; negotiation does not control implementation. | Requires evidence, credibility and enough independence to negotiate across parties. |
| C. Campaign on issues without endorsements. | Keeps participation open across voting choices. | Members may want a clearer electoral answer; parties can borrow the campaign. | Requires a real audience or organising base; hostile perception affects reach. |
| D. Permit individual electoral choices under existing rules. | Avoids forcing one party choice on the whole movement. | People and relationships can migrate into parties. | Apply actual membership, asset and name rules; no rules means greater exposure. |

D remains available, but is not an automatic absorption button. Strong collective governance can permit personal choices while retaining the institution. Likewise A cannot make a party out of a campaign announcement.

**E6:** individual transitions preserve the organisation / migration hollows it out / some leaders leave while a core remains / little changes. Actual independence, finance, governance and accumulated dependence shape probabilities. This draw modifies the year’s accumulated result; it cannot single-handedly determine the ending.

## Externality system

### Three layers, always in this order

1. Resolve the direct action: capacity, resources spent, obligations and broken relationships.
2. At a scheduled externality slot, calculate contextual weights and draw once.
3. Store the draw, explain the reaction, and carry the resulting situation forward.

Use six slots: 2A/2B, 4B (or the aftermath of 4A), 6, 9, 11 and 12. Some runs therefore have fewer surprising events. Do not roll again on rendering, browser resizing, revisiting an outcome or reopening a saved game.

### Authoring contract for every option

Each table row supplies action, direct benefit, adverse cost and a requirement with its delayed return. The option inherits its turn’s externality pool where one exists; otherwise the externality fields are “none scheduled”. Each implementation record must contain:

`action`, `playerDescription`, `directEffects`, `structuralCost`, `positiveExternality`, `negativeExternality`, `mixedExternality`, `quietExternality`, `probabilityModifiers`, `requirementCreated`, `delayedTurn`, `prerequisites`, `fallback`.

A favourable draw never refunds the structural cost automatically. An adverse draw never removes a capability without a causal event. Direct effects can be predictable without moral approval being certain.

### Initial probability recipe for playtesting

Start eligible slots at positive 25, negative 25, mixed 25, quiet 25. Apply relevant contextual modifiers to positive or negative weights, bounded to ±15 per factor; clamp each final weight between 5 and 60, then normalise. These are design seeds, not calibrated claims about politics.

| Context | Positive weight | Negative weight | Applies to |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Specific, publicly understood unfinished commitments | +10 | −5 | Occupation coverage |
| Widened demands without a mandate | −5 | +10 | Occupation, label |
| Functioning alliance and fulfilled reciprocal obligations | +10 | −5 | Coalition, protection |
| Partners excluded after earlier promises | −10 | +15 | Coalition, consultation |
| Demonstrated, verified school follow-through | +10 | −5 | Label, public reception of access |
| Clear public rules on party participation | +10 | −5 | Alliance perception, label, consultation |
| Repeated partisan financial/operational dependence | −5 | +10 | Proxy accusation, electoral migration |
| High public legitimacy | +5 | −5 | Interpretation of confrontation |
| Strong actual independence and member control | +10 | −10 | Retaining an institution through elections |

Use the pre-draw state after direct consequences. Do not let the current draw modify its own odds. Show narrative reasons (“your published settlement gave reporters something specific to check”), never the weights.

A positive reception, mixed reception and quiet outcome must be substantively distinct; avoid three phrasings of the same score change. Core intensity and participation breadth can move in opposite directions in the mixed result.

### Distinct internal concepts

- **Organisation:** ability to coordinate work, records and decisions.
- **Mobilisation:** ability to bring reachable people into collective action.
- **Participation breadth:** who can plausibly join; distinct from intensity among a committed core.
- **Public legitimacy:** perceived credibility and benefit of the doubt, not a simulation verdict on moral worth.
- **Agenda coherence:** whether claims connect and commitments remain intelligible.
- **Actual independence:** control of decisions, assets and funding.
- **Perceived independence:** whether audiences believe that control is real.
- **Alliance trust:** living relationships that can deteriorate, not a permanent acquired badge.
- **Accountability:** ability to authorise decisions and follow promises.
- **Money and operational capacity:** available resources, restrictions, staffing and time to complete work.

Service delivery can strengthen trust without setting ideology. Public hostility can reduce breadth without proving the movement wrong. A hostile allegation remains an allegation in every version of the narration.

### Dependency acceptance checks

- No legal option at 11 from “evidence” alone; a lawyer network must have been staffed and maintained.
- A lost alliance cannot reappear because its flag was once set.
- Governance is available only after enrolment, mandate or election work is completed.
- Funding restrictions apply to the work the money can actually support.
- Previously widened mandates return as obligations; retreat remains possible but visible.
- Police records at Turn 1 return as a reason to protect case data and participation at 3A/4B, 6 and 11. Never require players to publish sensitive student lists to prove transparency.
- Both branches explicitly close the occupation before school work, with different outstanding commitments.
- Every capability gate has an honest fallback; the game never deadlocks.
- Identical actions with different seeds may produce different reactions, but identical seed plus actions reproduces the same year.
- Repeated draws cannot make the final turn dominate eleven turns of construction.

## Ending

Lead with THRIVED, RELEVANT, SURVIVED or ABSORBED. Follow with institutional form and three or four causal decisions, plus one material external reaction if it substantially affected the result. Distinguish “you chose” from “the response you encountered”. Absorption can occur from strength. Survival means continued existence, not proof of political usefulness.
